---
title: "Most of the work is not the model"
date: "2026-01-15"
readTime: "4 min"
excerpt: "The model is maybe 10% of an AI product. The other 90% is where it gets won or lost..."
---

When people sketch an AI product they draw the model in the center, big and glowing, and everything else as little boxes around the edge. The real proportions are closer to the opposite. The model is maybe ten percent of the work. The other ninety percent is the unglamorous machinery around it, and that is where the product is actually won or lost.

Think about what is left once you accept that the model is a given. You did not train it. You are not going to out-train it. So your edge has to come from everywhere else.

The data you feed it. What you retrieve, how you chunk it, what you decide is relevant. Get this wrong and the smartest model in the world answers the wrong question beautifully.

The evals. The harness that tells you whether a change made things better or just different. Without it you are tuning by vibes, and vibes do not survive contact with real users.

The interface. How the person asks, what they see while they wait, how a wrong answer is caught and corrected. A mediocre model behind a thoughtful interface beats a great model behind a clumsy one, almost every time.

The guardrails. What the system refuses to do, what it does when it is unsure, what happens at the edges where confident nonsense lives.

None of these have anything to do with model architecture, and all of them decide whether the thing is good. This is oddly freeing once it sinks in. It means the leverage is in reach. You do not need a frontier lab to build a genuinely good AI product. You need taste about data, discipline about evals, and care about the interface and the edges.

The model is the engine. Engines have mostly become a commodity you can buy. The car around it, the part people actually touch, is still yours to get right. Spend your time there.
